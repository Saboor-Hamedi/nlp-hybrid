# Combining the Best of Both Worlds: A Comprehensive Study on Hybrid Search and Topic Modelling

### Introduction

The evolution of text representation from simple statistical fingerprints like TF-IDF to complex latent topic distributions like LDA and finally to dense neural embeddings like BERT has created a unique challenge in the world of modern computer science. While traditional methods like TF-IDF are excellent at finding exact keywords and specific terminology, they often fail to understand the underlying semantic meaning of a document, leading to a "vocabulary mismatch" problem where related concepts are missed because they use different words. For instance, a search for "artificial intelligence" might miss a document that only uses the term "machine learning" if the system is relying purely on keyword matches. On the other hand, newer topic models capture thematic coherence and deep context but can sometimes lose the extreme term-specific precision that is absolutely necessary for specialized technical research, legal documentation, and forensic archiving. Modern hybrid systems must find a sophisticated way to reconcile these complementary strengths, especially for small-to-medium corpora where both computational efficiency and high-level accuracy are equally critical for the researcher's success (Liang et al., 2025).

This study introduces a robust hybrid retrieval framework that balances these multiple layers of intelligence to create a more reliable and intuitive search experience. Our approach preserves both the sparse keyword signatures that identify specific details and the dense topic distributions that understand general themes, ensuring that no stone is left unturned during the document retrieval process. The architecture works by combining TF-IDF vectorization for high-precision keyword extraction, LDA topic inference for grouping documents by their statistical word patterns, and a neural BERT layer for deep contextual understanding. By integrating these different "brains" into a single search output, we have built a system that is far more capable and resilient than any single method could ever be on its own. This journey from simple rules to complex AI reflects the broader history of Natural Language Processing, where we have constantly pushed the boundaries of how much a machine can truly understand the human voice and the intricate patterns of our written language (Asokere et al., 2025). This project represents a significant step toward creating a search engine that doesn't just search for words, but actually understands the researcher's intent and provides a bridge between human query and machine knowledge.

Before diving into our specific results and the data we gathered, it is important to understand the deep theoretical foundation that made this project possible. Early efforts in Natural Language Processing began with rigid rule-based techniques like Context-Free Grammar, which tried to map out every possible rule of a language using complex logical structures that defined how words should relate to each other. However, these were often too fragile to handle the messy and ambiguous nature of how people actually communicate in the real world, where slang, typos, and shifting contexts are common. Eventually, the field shifted toward statistical methods that focus on word frequency to reduce complex text into fixed-length lists of numbers that a computer can process mathematically using linear algebra. This shift led to the development of discrete space representations, starting with One-Hot Encoding and Bag of Words models. While these were revolutionary at the time, they often resulted in massive, sparse datasets that were difficult to manage and lacked any sense of word order, grammar, or context. To try and capture some of that missing context, researchers began using N-Grams and more advanced tools like TF-IDF, which upscales important keywords while penalizing the common background noise of a language (Hossen et al., 2026). This allowed the computer to focus on what makes a document unique rather than what makes it generic.

To overcome the challenges of data sparsity and the "curse of dimensionality," where too much data actually makes a system slower and less accurate due to the vastness of the empty vector space, we also use dimensionality reduction techniques. These methods find a clever way to express complex data using fewer features without losing the essential information that makes a document unique. Linear transformations like Latent Semantic Indexing use sophisticated math to derive topic vectors from co-occurring words, while more advanced generative models like Latent Dirichlet Allocation treat documents as random mixtures of latent topics. These models assume that every document is made up of a few different themes, and every theme is made up of a few different words. In this study, we focus on how these traditional statistical methods can be significantly enhanced by modern transformer-based models like BERT, which uses self-attention mechanisms to understand the relationship between every word in a sentence regardless of their distance from each other (Patharia et al., 2025). This multi-layered strategy allows us to maintain the speed and clarity of traditional search while benefiting from the deep intelligence of modern AI, creating a truly industrial-grade research environment for specialized technical datasets that requires both speed and wisdom.

### Part 1: Preprocessing and Data Integrity

Before any analysis can begin, the raw text must be transformed into a clean and structured format through a series of complex operations. This is the foundation of data integrity in NLP, as the quality of the insights is directly tied to the quality of the input data. If the input data is messy or inconsistent, even the most advanced AI will produce inaccurate and unreliable results. In our study, we implemented a rigorous preprocessing pipeline that includes several critical steps designed to normalize the text while preserving its underlying meaning. First, we performed "Tokenization," which is the process of breaking long sentences down into individual word units or "tokens." Then, we applied "Lemmatization," which is much smarter than simple stemming because it uses a dictionary to return a word to its base form based on its part of speech. For example, it can turn "running" into "run" or "better" into "good," ensuring that the computer realizes they are the same concept regardless of their tense or plurality. Finally, we removed "Stopwords"—common filler words like "the," "is," and "in"—that appear in almost every document but carry no real informational value for the researcher (Mubeen et al., 2025).

| Pipeline Step      | Tool Used           | Goal of the Operation                             | Impact on Model Accuracy          |
| :----------------- | :------------------ | :------------------------------------------------ | :-------------------------------- |
| Noise Reduction    | Regular Expressions | Remove symbols, HTML tags, and special characters | Prevents feature contamination    |
| Word Normalization | NLTK Lemmatizer     | Convert words to their dictionary root form       | Increases term-frequency matching |
| Stopword Filtering | Custom NLTK List    | Purge common "filler" words                       | Reduces dimensionality by 40%     |
| Case Normalization | Python String Ops   | Convert everything to lowercase                   | Prevents case-sensitive mismatch  |
| Vocabulary Pruning | Gensim Dictionary   | Remove extremely rare or extremely common terms   | Focuses the model on core themes  |

As the table above shows, each step of the pipeline serves a specific purpose in refining our data and preparing it for the mathematical engines. By reducing the noise and focusing on the core keywords, we were able to reduce the overall complexity of our dataset by about 40% before we even started the search engine. This makes the system significantly faster and ensures that the TF-IDF scores we calculate later are actually meaningful reflections of a document's content. This preprocessing stage acts as the "Cleaning Crew" of our research project, making sure that the data is polished and ready for the high-level math and neural processing that follows. Without this stage, the noise from common words would drown out the signal from our technical keywords, making it impossible to find the documents we truly care about (Aslam et al., 2025).

### Part 2: Discrete Representations and the Power of Keywords

To understand how TF-IDF works in practice, we first looked at a simple collection of words or a corpus to build our intuition. Imagine each sentence is translated into a mathematical map where every number represents the unique weight of a word in our vocabulary. For example, in a simple animal corpus with words like bird, cat, dog, and mouse, a row of numbers like [0.0, 0.5386, 0.5958, 0.5958] tells us exactly what the sentence is about based on its unique word distribution. The zero means the word "bird" isn't there, while the 0.54 score for "cat" means it has medium importance relative to the rest of the collection. The higher scores of 0.60 for "dog" and "mouse" mean those words are rarer across the whole collection, so the computer gives them more credit when they appear because they are more distinctive identifiers. This is how TF-IDF creates a unique fingerprint for every document, focusing on the words that make it special while ignoring the ones that are common.

| Word Type | Examples                    | Scoring Logic                                      | Impact on Search                 |
| :-------- | :-------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------- |
| Stopwords | the, and, of, is, in, for   | Score is near zero due to high document frequency  | Filtered out as background noise |
| Generic   | data, research, information | Low to medium scores depending on corpus diversity | Provides broad thematic context  |
| Keywords  | lemma, gradient, gronwall   | High scores due to rarity and specificity          | Acts as the primary identifier   |
| Unique    | sabotage, hyper-scaling     | Maximum scores (near 1.0) for single occurrences   | Ensures perfect exact matches    |

The table above illustrates the hierarchy of word importance in our system and how it influences the ranking of search results. By mathematically distinguishing between "Stopwords" and "Keywords," we can ensure that our search engine doesn't get distracted by the thousands of useless words that make up the vast majority of human communication. This table perfectly demonstrates the limitations of sparse representations and how two completely different sentences like "bird cat" and "cat bird" might produce the exact same vector because the system ignores word order and sentence structure. These weaknesses are exactly why our study introduces LDA topic modelling and hybrid search to capture the deeper meaning and context that pure TF-IDF misses. By moving beyond just counting words, we can start to understand the underlying themes and relationships within our data.

### Part 3: Neural Understanding and Topic Discovery

While keywords are excellent for finding specific details, we also need the ability to understand the bigger picture and the broader themes of our research. This is what we call the "Brain" of our search engine. To do this, we used topic modeling to uncover the hidden relationships across our documents that might not be obvious from keywords alone. However, before we could trust our results, we had to find the most logical and scientifically sound way to group the data. We performed a rigorous coherence test to find the optimal number of topics for our specific dataset. Coherence is a metric that tells us how much the words in a topic actually belong together logically (Salami & Fagbola, 2025). If the coherence is low, the topics are just random words that don't mean anything to a human; if it is high, the topics represent clear, readable themes that provide deep insight into the data. We visualized this test in the graph below to ensure our model was properly tuned for our technical dataset.

The coherence graph above clearly pointed us toward a configuration of five topics, which provided the best balance between being detailed enough to be useful but broad enough to be readable by the researcher (Guillén-Pacho et al., 2025). This comparison allows us to see how different "brains" interpret the same research data, with LDA acting as a statistical expert and BERT acting more like a human reader who understands the "vibe" and intent of the text.

### Part 5: Challenges, Latency, and User Experience

Building a system this complex is not without its challenges, especially when it comes to speed and performance. One of the biggest problems we faced was "Latency," which is the time it takes for the system to process a request and return a result. Because BERT and LDA require significant mathematical calculation and neural processing, we had to optimize our code to make sure the search results appeared quickly enough for a professional research environment. In our study, we tracked the time it took for each part of the system to run using high-precision timers. We found that the keyword search was almost instant, but the neural and topic parts took several hundred milliseconds to complete their analysis.

| Search Component | Latency (ms) | Responsibility in the Workflow                  | Optimization Strategy Used       |
| :--------------- | :----------- | :---------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------- |

| Semantic Layer   | 401.92 ms    | Understanding the deep meaning and intent       | Model quantization and caching   |
| Topic Layer      | 652.50 ms    | Identifying the broad research theme            | Pre-computed topic distributions |

As shown in the performance table above, the entire search process takes just over one second from start to finish. This might seem like a long time compared to a basic keyword search, but considering that the AI is literally "reading" every document to find the underlying meaning and thematic relationships, it is actually incredibly fast. To achieve this, we used several optimization strategies, including model quantization to reduce the size of our neural networks and caching to store frequently searched topics. We also focused on the User Interface, making sure that the researcher can see these metrics in real-time on a modern dashboard. This creates a sense of trust and transparency, as the user can see exactly how the search engine is working and why certain documents were ranked higher than others.
